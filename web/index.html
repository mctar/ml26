Moonlander

Roadster edition

Because of changes in the national policy, the lunar exploratory program is once again functioning, and you have been chosen as the pilot. You mission is to land on Mare Fremont, a barren and harsh wasteland believed incapable of supporting any known form of life. The agency chose the best of the last of the human-driven cars. Its onboard computer is a PDP-11, and it has just died on you. You must now land under manual control.

Leaving solar orbit for the Moon

Moonlander, Roadster edition

Jack Burness wrote Moonlander in 1973 for DEC's GT40, a PDP-11 with a vector display and a light pen. This is that program: recovered, translated routine by routine into Rust, proved against the original binary step for step, and compiled to run here. The physics, the rules, the terrain, the messages and the fuel budget are his, unchanged. What you see is 2026.

The car

A Roadster, the one-seat kind that is meant to leave the ground, coming down on compressed air. It weighs 10,086 lb because that is the number the 1973 code divides by, and that number is an address, not a weight. Nobody has published a weight for the real one either.

The ground

The terrain is Burness's table at true scale, which makes it a range of needles up to 13,282 feet tall. The program measures your height against that table until you are below 450 feet; then it switches to its close-up and treats the ground as a quarter of its height, and the 1973 display cut to a new picture at that moment so that nobody noticed. This version keeps both heights and cuts the camera in the same place. The mountain the readout says you are 10,000 feet above is the mountain you hit.

Controls

Drag the thrust bar, or use the up and down keys. The arrows turn; Shift makes them the big arrows. Under the hood every one of these moves a light pen onto the controls Burness drew, and the program reads nothing but the pen. V shows the 1973 tube. R starts again. S copies a link that replays this flight exactly.
